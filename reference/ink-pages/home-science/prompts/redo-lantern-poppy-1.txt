The FIRST attached painting is the companion picture of this one: paint the very same lantern, by the same painter in the same colours, so the two belong together as a pair. (The second attached painting, a mailbox, is only the brush-hand reference.)

STYLE: A small, quiet watercolour for a California brand's website, in loose thin watercolour on plain white paper. Use the attached painting (a country mailbox) only as the reference for the brush hand (do not paint what it shows): the same painter, the same few wet washes with soft bleeding edges, a few dry-brush streaks, very few strokes, lots of bare white paper. The more it looks like a real watercolour painting, the better. Not photo-real, not a detailed drawing, not a storybook or children's-book illustration, not a cartoon, no pen outlines, no flat cut-out shapes.

It is an American, California subject, in the spirit of the California plein-air watercolour painters, NOT an Asian ink painting: no characters, no seal or stamp.

PALETTE: soft blue-grey, a warm weathered sienna, a little sage green and dry-grass straw, all thin and pale, on pure white paper. The parts named below as gold are real gold leaf: flat and matte, a warm deep gold, not yellow, not glowing.

COMPOSITION: Landscape, 1536 x 1024. One subject, centred, filling about two thirds of the frame, with clear bare white paper on every side. Its edges and the ground under it dissolve softly into the paper; nothing is cut off by the frame; no frame, no border, no background scene, no sky wash. The ground is only a loose pale wash.

NEVER: no people, no hands, no text, letters, numbers or writing anywhere, no signature, no red, no orange flames, no glow, no halo, no glitter, no lens flare.

HOW LOOSE: paint it in just a few wet washes. Big soft shapes that bleed at the edge, nothing outlined, no fine detail, no rivets, speckle or wood grain. If a part can be left to bare paper, leave it. From across a room, and small on a phone, it must still read at once.

THE SUBJECT: The same old kerosene barn lantern as in the first attached painting, the same three-quarter view, lit, large in the frame and set a little left of centre, on the same pale ground. Its glass globe is plain clear glass exactly as in the attached painting: NO wire guard, no cage, no wires, bands or rings across the glass, nothing wrapped around it. Leave out the dry grass: the ground is only a bare pale wash.

Inside the glass, around the flame, the flame is throwing sparks: about twelve sparks, each a flat little leaf-shaped fleck of real gold leaf, clearly bigger than a dot, scattered all through the inside of the globe. Every one of them is plainly INSIDE the glass, held in by it; several rest right against the inside of the glass where it has stopped them.

Only three sparks have got out. They have risen out from under the lantern's cap at the top and fallen in one clear arc to the right, onto a California poppy that stands just to the right of the lantern, large, about half the lantern's height. The poppy has been scorched by them: two of its orange petals have ragged black burnt holes and blackened curled edges (black ink only, never red), one burn still giving off a thin grey wisp of smoke, with a spark of gold lying in it; one spark is still in the air above the flower. At the far left of the lantern stands the other poppy, fresh and whole, for contrast. The flame and the sparks are the only gold. The idea the picture must tell, readable in two seconds with no words: the flame throws sparks, the glass holds most of them in, and the few that get out do harm.
